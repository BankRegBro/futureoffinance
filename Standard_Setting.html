Assessed once, trusted often

A modest standard-setting body, seeded by the FDIC in 2026, has done what a decade of guidance could not: made it economically rational for a small bank to try something new.

Cedar Falls, Iowa, and Washington, D.C. · Filed from 2032

The product that Farmers & Merchants State Bank launched in March was not, by the standards of financial technology, revolutionary. It was a cash-flow underwriting tool that reads a small business's bank transaction history and produces a working-capital line decision in under a day, the sort of thing large banks have offered since the late 2020s and fintech lenders since before that. What was remarkable was not the product. It was that a bank with $410m in assets and thirty-one employees was able to deploy it at all.

"Six weeks," says Lisa Brandt, the bank's president, with the particular satisfaction of someone quoting a number she has quoted many times. "Signed the contract in January, live in March. Diligence took nine days."

Brandt has been running the bank for eleven years. She remembers the alternative. In 2024 she looked at a broadly comparable vendor and abandoned the project after four months. The problem was never the software. The problem was that evaluating whether the vendor met her regulators' expectations for third-party risk management required a review her bank could not perform and could not afford to buy. The vendor's annual licence was $34,000. The outside diligence quote was $118,000. "You do that arithmetic once," she says, "and then you stop looking at vendors."

Multiply that arithmetic across roughly four thousand community banks, and you have a reasonable description of why, for most of the 2020s, technological innovation in American banking was something that happened at large institutions and to everyone else.

The duplication problem

The economics that defeated Brandt in 2024 were not a market failure in any exotic sense. They were a straightforward coordination problem, and everyone involved could see it.

Banking regulators require institutions to conduct due diligence on third-party service providers proportionate to the risk those providers introduce. The requirement is sound. Its practical effect, in a fragmented banking system, was that the same fintech vendor would be independently reviewed by every bank that considered using it. Each review covered substantially the same ground, produced substantially the same conclusions, and cost the vendor weeks of engineering and compliance time and the bank tens of thousands of dollars.

For a large institution spreading that cost across a $2trn balance sheet, this was an irritation. For a community bank it was a prohibition. The fixed cost of establishing that a vendor was safe did not scale down with the size of the bank doing the establishing. A $400m bank paid roughly what a $400bn bank paid, for the same review, and then absorbed it against a fraction of the revenue.

The vendors, for their part, were being asked the same several hundred questions repeatedly by institutions that would not share the answers with each other. Small fintech firms routinely reported that responding to bank diligence questionnaires consumed more staff time than product development. Many simply stopped selling to community banks, concluding that the acquisition cost per institution could never be recovered on the contract sizes available.

"You do that arithmetic once, and then you stop looking at vendors."

The collapse of Synapse in April 2024 gave the problem a face. The banking-as-a-service middleware firm's implosion left consumers unable to access roughly $90m in funds held through partner banks, and exposed how little several of those banks had actually understood about the ledgering arrangements they were relying on. The failure was not caused by an absence of diligence requirements. It was caused, in part, by diligence that was extensive on paper, duplicated across institutions, and shallow in the places that turned out to matter.

That combination, burdensome and inadequate at the same time, is the specific pathology that a standard-setting body is designed to cure.

What the FDIC built

The idea had been circulating since the first Trump administration, when then-FDIC Chairman Jelena McWilliams floated a certification regime for bank technology vendors. It went nowhere for several years, resurfacing periodically in speeches and comment letters without ever acquiring an owner.

It acquired one in the summer of 2026. A draft term sheet circulated by the FDIC in July of that year proposed an independent standard-setting organisation, convened with the American Bankers Association, the Independent Community Bankers of America, the Bank Policy Institute, the Financial Technology Association, the American Fintech Council, and the Coalition for Financial Ecosystem Standards. The FDIC would provide seed funding. The OCC joined within weeks. The body that emerged, incorporated the following year as the Bank Innovation Standards Council and universally known as BISC, held its first standards vote in 2028.

The design choices made in that first year explain most of what has happened since.

The first was that BISC would write standards and accredit assessors, but would not itself conduct assessments. The council publishes the criteria. Independent assessor firms, accredited by BISC and ring-fenced from any consulting relationship with the vendors they review, perform the work. Results are published to a certification registry that any bank can consult. The separation was deliberate and, in the view of most participants, is the reason the certifications carry weight. A standard-setter that also sold assessments would have had an obvious incentive problem.

The second choice was that certification would be voluntary and would confer no safe harbour. This was the most contested provision in the term sheet and the one the industry most wanted changed. Banks argued, not unreasonably, that if a vendor had been assessed against federally acknowledged standards by an accredited assessor, contracting with that vendor ought to reduce a bank's own supervisory exposure. The regulators refused. Banks remain responsible for safe and sound operation, for consumer protection, for ongoing monitoring, and for institution-specific risk assessment. A certificate is evidence. It is not a defence.

In retrospect the refusal was the making of the thing. A safe harbour would have converted certification into a compliance formality, a document to be obtained and filed. Its absence kept banks engaged in the parts of diligence that are genuinely institution-specific: how the vendor integrates with this bank's systems, what this bank's contract says, what this bank monitors after go-live. BISC standardised the questions that have the same answer at every bank. It left alone the questions that do not.

The arithmetic, revisited

The effect on community bank technology adoption has been larger than most of the framework's designers expected. The core mechanism is simple: a common assessment, performed once, refreshed on a defined cycle, and reused by every bank that consults the registry. A vendor assessed in January is available to four thousand institutions in February on the strength of the same review. The fixed cost that could not be scaled down is instead scaled across.

What those banks are deploying is the more interesting question. The early expectation was that community banks would use the new capacity to buy the things large banks already had: better online account opening, mobile deposit, digital lending workflows. A good deal of that has happened, and it has largely closed the customer-experience gap that was driving deposit attrition to national institutions.

But a second pattern has emerged that few anticipated. Community banks have begun deploying technology that large banks have no particular reason to build, because it serves markets too small or too specific to interest them. Agricultural cash-flow forecasting tools calibrated to individual commodity cycles. Tribal lending platforms designed around trust-land title complexities that national underwriting models cannot process. Multilingual account-opening flows for immigrant communities in specific metropolitan corridors. Remittance corridors serving single sending regions.

"The thing nobody predicted," says Todd Phillips, a former FDIC official now at the Klaros Group, "is that lowering the diligence cost didn't just let small banks buy the same products as big banks. It made it viable for vendors to build products for small banks. If you can sell to four thousand institutions on one certification instead of negotiating four thousand separate reviews, suddenly the community bank market is a market. Before, it was a collection of four thousand individually unprofitable sales cycles."

The examiners adjusted

The framework's effect on supervision has been quieter and, in the assessment of the agencies themselves, largely positive. Examiners were explicitly instructed that certification confers no safe harbour and that third-party risk management remains a full-scope examination topic. In practice, the existence of a common baseline changed what examiners spent their time on. A bank using a certified vendor does not need to demonstrate that the vendor has adequate encryption at rest, business continuity planning, or subservicer oversight. Those are covered by the certification and verifiable from the registry. The examination conversation moves directly to the questions that are actually specific to the institution: contract terms, integration architecture, monitoring, exit planning.

State banking regulators, who supervise the majority of community banks, were early and forceful advocates. The Conference of State Bank Supervisors pushed hard during the term sheet negotiations for the registry to be publicly accessible rather than subscription-gated, on the grounds that a certification regime affordable only to institutions that could pay for access would reproduce the inequity it was meant to solve. The provision survived. The registry is free to consult.

The assessor market has been the framework's most awkward growing pain. BISC accredited eleven assessor firms in its first cycle. Three subsequently lost accreditation, one for inadequate testing depth and two for conflict-of-interest violations involving advisory relationships with assessed vendors. The council's enforcement was swift and public, which most observers regard as having been essential to the registry's credibility. It also revealed that accrediting assessors is a harder ongoing task than writing standards, and that BISC's budget was initially calibrated for the latter rather than the former.

What certification does not do

In February 2030 a certified payments vendor serving roughly ninety community banks failed. The failure was operational rather than fraudulent, a botched migration that corrupted transaction records and took eleven days to fully reconstruct, but it was disruptive, expensive, and it happened to a firm carrying a current BISC certification.

The post-mortem was uncomfortable and, in the end, clarifying. The assessment had been performed correctly against the standards as written. The standards, at that time, did not adequately address change-management controls during major infrastructure migrations. The vendor had passed a test that did not test the thing that broke.

BISC amended the standard within four months and required re-certification against the new criterion on an accelerated cycle. The banks affected recovered. But the episode established, in a way that no amount of drafting could have, what a certification actually is: evidence that a vendor met a defined set of criteria at a point in time, assessed by a qualified third party. It is not a guarantee of performance, and the standards will always lag the failure modes nobody has seen yet.

"The certificate tells you the vendor passed the test," Brandt says. "It doesn't tell you the test covered everything. We still ask our own questions. We just don't ask the four hundred questions that somebody else already asked and published the answers to."

The shape of the unlock

It is worth being precise about what has and has not changed, because the community banking sector has been the subject of a great deal of premature obituary-writing and a certain amount of premature celebration.

Consolidation has not stopped. The number of insured depository institutions continues to decline, driven by succession problems, margin compression, and most acutely by the cybersecurity cost burden that no standard-setting body addresses. BISC has not made community banking easy. It has removed one specific constraint.

But it was a load-bearing constraint. The community bank's durable advantage has always been proximity: knowing the borrower, the collateral, the local economy, the difference between a bad year and a bad business. What the sector lost over two decades was not that advantage but the ability to pair it with competent technology. The diligence-cost barrier meant that a bank could either be small and local or technologically current, and the arithmetic pushed hard toward the former.

Brandt's bank is now doing something that would have been unavailable to it in 2024: applying a machine-learning cash-flow model to underwriting decisions that she then overrides, roughly one time in nine, on the basis of knowledge the model does not have. "The model is better than me on the numbers," she says. "I'm better than the model on whether the numbers mean what they appear to mean. That combination is the whole business. I just couldn't get to it before, because getting to it cost more than the bank could pay."

That is a modest description of a significant thing. The standard-setting body seeded by the FDIC in 2026 did not transform banking. It made a category of decision affordable that had previously been priced out of reach for four thousand institutions, and then those institutions did what they were always good at, with better tools.

Not every regulatory intervention needs to be more ambitious than that.
