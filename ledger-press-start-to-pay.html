Press start to pay

Sony has asked the OCC for a national trust charter. If Comptroller Gould grants it, an entertainment company will hold bank powers and issue its own regulated dollar. The arithmetic is compelling. The precedents for this kind of venture are almost uniformly bad.

Sony's businesses currently clear through separate processors that do not talk to each other. A single issued dollar collapses them onto one rail, retains the interchange, and holds the float on the way through. Figures are stylised.

The filing arrived at the Office of the Comptroller of the Currency in late 2026, forty-seven pages of dense regulatory prose submitted under the name Sony Financial Trust, N.A. Applications for national trust charters are not ordinarily newsworthy. This one is different. Buried in section four, under the heading "Proposed Activities and Services," is a single sentence that has set the payments industry on edge: the applicant intends, upon approval and subject to certification under the GENIUS Act of 2026, to issue a fully reserved, US dollar-denominated payment stablecoin for use across Sony's global digital ecosystem.

Sony is, in other words, applying to become a bank. Not a retail bank. It is not proposing to take deposits from the public or make loans in any conventional sense. A trust bank: a narrowly chartered institution holding assets for others, and in this case holding the Treasury-bill reserves that would back a Sony-issued dollar circulating across PlayStation Network, Sony Pictures, Sony Music, the Bravia platform, and a constellation of affiliated services that together touch an estimated 700 million people.

The OCC has not ruled. Comptroller Gould has made little secret of his disposition, describing the GENIUS Act in an autumn speech as the most consequential piece of financial infrastructure legislation since Dodd-Frank and arguing that his mandate under it is to enable regulated innovation rather than to protect incumbent business models. People familiar with the review expect a conditional approval in the first quarter of 2027.

"We are modelling the approval as a near-certainty," says one senior executive at a large card-issuing bank, who asked not to be named. "The question is not whether it happens. It is what we do when it does."

The arithmetic that makes it worth doing

Consider what Sony controls. PlayStation Network has 145 million monthly users spending an estimated $26bn a year on games, content, subscriptions and in-game purchases. Sony Pictures releases roughly 25 films a year into a library reaching over 100 countries. Sony Music is one of three major labels. The Alpha camera ecosystem sustains a community that buys hardware, software subscriptions and cloud storage on recurring cycles. Bravia televisions are the primary screen in tens of millions of households.

Each of these currently handles payments through a different mixture of cards, regional processors and platform wallets that do not communicate. A customer who games, streams, buys music and stores photographs pays for each separately, through separate interfaces, generating interchange of two to three percent every time. A single issued dollar collapses all of it onto one rail.

The interchange saving is the obvious prize and the smaller one: roughly $520m a year at full adoption. The float is the real business. Under the GENIUS Act's reserve requirements an issuer holds one-to-one backing in short-duration Treasuries, and the Act permits the issuer to retain the yield on them. The consumer holding a balance in a Sony wallet is lending Sony money that Sony invests in government securities and keeps the return on.

The rewards structure is the elegant part. SEN Credits would be distributed as a fraction of the yield earned on a holder's own balance, redeemable for game content, subscriptions and hardware discounts. A consumer keeping $150 in the wallet earns something like $6.45 a year. Modest individually, and structurally more durable than the interchange-funded points programmes it competes with, because Sony is not paying for the rewards out of margin. It is handing back a slice of the float and keeping the rest.

"It is the most elegant monetisation of trust I have seen proposed in years," says Thomas Osei, a fintech investor tracking the stablecoin ecosystem since the Act passed. "They take the consumer's own money, put it in Treasuries, and give back part of the return as entertainment rewards. The consumer feels rewarded. And leaving costs them the reward stream."

Why a trust charter

The choice of instrument reflects a calculation about control. A state money-transmitter licence is a fifty-jurisdiction patchwork that constrains reserve composition. A bank partnership hands oversight and margin to the chartered partner. A national trust charter gives Sony a federally regulated institution of its own, valid everywhere, able to hold and manage the reserve without intermediation and to issue an instrument carrying the legitimacy of a nationally chartered bank.

The banking lobby objects, volubly. The Independent Community Bankers of America has filed a comment letter arguing that trust charters for technology conglomerates create an uneven field, delivering the benefits of a charter without the obligations of full-service banking. The letter has the ring of an argument made by people who know they are losing and feel obliged to make the record.

The more substantive concern, reportedly raised inside the OCC itself, is data. A trust entity intermediating payments across gaming, streaming, music, film and photography accumulates a dataset of extraordinary granularity: not what consumers enjoy, which platform analytics already reveal, but what they will pay for it, how fast they spend after loading, and how their leisure correlates with their financial behaviour. Bank privacy rules were written long before anyone contemplated an entertainment-conglomerate bank. Any conditional approval is expected to carry enhanced data-governance requirements and a hard separation between payment data and commercial data.

The Tokyo question

There is a dimension of this application that has attracted less attention than it deserves, which is that the applicant's parent is Japanese.

A national trust charter would sit inside a corporate group whose ultimate supervision belongs to the Japan Financial Services Agency, and the OCC's assessment has to reach questions that do not arise for a domestic applicant. Whether the reserve is genuinely ring-fenced from the parent in an insolvency governed by Japanese law. Whether US supervisors can obtain what they need from a group whose books sit largely outside their reach. Whether the FSA and the OCC can agree a supervisory understanding, and how quickly, given that the FSA has its own developing stablecoin regime and its own view about a domestic champion issuing dollars.

None of this is disqualifying. Foreign-owned institutions hold US charters routinely. But it adds a layer of negotiation that the application's timeline has not obviously priced in, and it gives the OCC a legitimate reason to move slowly that has nothing to do with protecting Visa.

The graveyard

Everything above is the bull case, and it is genuinely strong. It is also worth remembering that the history of large companies issuing their own payment instruments is close to a graveyard.

A consortium of the largest American retailers spent years and a great deal of money building a merchant-controlled payment network specifically to escape interchange, and it never launched at scale. Retailer-branded wallets have been introduced repeatedly across two decades and have almost uniformly failed to achieve meaningful penetration. The reason is consistent and unglamorous: a general-purpose card works everywhere, earns rewards on everything, and extends credit when the customer needs it, while a single-merchant wallet does one thing in one place and demands the customer maintain a separate balance to do it.

"Every one of these has died the same death. Not rejection. Indifference. People simply do not want a second wallet for one shop."

Sony's answer is that its ecosystem is unusually large, unusually sticky, and unusually good at making its customers feel they are inside something rather than shopping at it. That answer is more credible than most of its predecessors. It is still the same bet those predecessors made, and the base rate is unkind.

There is also the matter of Sony's own security history, which the charter review cannot avoid and which the company's public case conspicuously does not address. The 2011 PlayStation Network intrusion exposed the personal data of roughly 77 million accounts and took the service offline for weeks. In 2014 an intrusion at Sony Pictures destroyed internal systems, drained the company's private correspondence into public view, and became the reference case for what a determined state-linked actor can do to an entertainment company that is not a bank.

Neither event says anything definitive about the security of a trust entity chartered in 2027 and built for the purpose. Both say something about institutional history, and prudential supervision is substantially the practice of taking institutional history seriously. An applicant asking to hold billions in reserves and to become the payment identity for tens of millions of young customers will be asked, in detail, what changed.

What could go wrong

The failure modes are worth setting out plainly, because the commentary so far has been split between people who think this is inevitable and people who think it is outrageous, and very few who have worked through how it actually breaks.

The generational prize

What unsettles the payments industry is not the arithmetic. It is the demography.

The cohort Sony is targeting skews heavily to the 18-to-35 range, which is precisely when primary financial habits form. Visa and Mastercard built their franchises by being present at the first credit card application. Sony proposes to be present earlier, at the moment a young adult's entertainment spending, which for this group is frequent and substantial, moves onto a payment system native to the platform they already live inside.

"The card became the default because it was the first one," says a senior executive at one of the major networks, who also declined to be named. "If they get the wallet relationship at twenty-two, the card relationship at twenty-five is a different conversation. That is what keeps us up at night."

The cross-media linkages amplify it. A wallet loaded for a game offers a discounted month of streaming. A music subscription paid in SEN Coin earns credits redeemable for PlayStation content. None of it requires the customer to think of themselves as using a financial product. The payment is invisible, the rewards accumulate, and the loop tightens.

The flywheel and the lawyers

The most ambitious element, and the one drawing scrutiny alongside the charter review, is what Sony's strategists call the content flywheel. Adoption is accelerated by content incentives available only to wallet holders. Broader adoption means a larger float and more reserve income. Reserve income funds more content and more generous rewards, which drives further adoption. The financial infrastructure and the creative infrastructure stop being parallel businesses that share a parent and become one engine.

Sony has been careful in public. The filing describes content incentives as optional commercial promotions rather than structural features of the payment product. Competition lawyers note that the line between a promotional tie and an anticompetitive one turns on implementation: offering wallet holders a discount is not the same as withholding content from everyone else, and Sony has explicitly disclaimed the latter. Whether the commercial pressure to maximise adoption preserves that distinction over time is a question the Department of Justice has reportedly begun to ask.

The road to a ruling

A decision is expected in the first quarter. The Stablecoin Certification Commission typically follows an OCC trust approval within ninety days where the reserve architecture has already been examined, which would put an instrument in consumers' hands before the end of the year.

The form matters as much as the fact. A narrow charter restricting the coin to Sony's own services validates the immediate commercial logic and defers the real question. A broad charter authorising third-party merchant acceptance makes Sony a payment network competitor on day one, and the networks do not believe their responses are ready for that.

The remaining avenue of resistance is Congress. Several members of the Senate Banking Committee have signalled interest in amending the Act to impose holding periods before float income can be retained, CRA-equivalent obligations, and caps on the circulating supply of any single non-bank issuer. Whether anything passes before Gould rules is doubtful. The calendar is crowded and the fintech lobby is well resourced.

What is not in doubt is that the GENIUS Act created a framework under which a consumer electronics and entertainment company can plausibly obtain the powers of a federally chartered institution and issue its own dollar. The framework exists. Sony is testing it first, and the answer will determine the future of every large consumer platform now watching.

There are, by the OCC's own count, eleven such pre-applications under informal discussion with licensing staff. Whether any of them is worth filing depends on something no regulator will decide, which is whether ordinary people can be persuaded to keep money somewhere new.
