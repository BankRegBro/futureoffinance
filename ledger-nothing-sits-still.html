Nothing sits still

Open banking handed the keys to software. Agentic commerce gave that software a reason to move money every day. What is left of the deposit franchise is a spot market, and the banks are bidding against machines.

The outflow at Buckeye Commerce Bank began at 9.12 on a Tuesday morning and was substantially over by lunch. There was no queue, no rumour, no viral post, and no moment at which anybody panicked. A competitor two states away had repriced its savings product by 40 basis points at nine o'clock. By 9.12 the first agents had finished their arithmetic. By 1.30 the bank had lost 14 percent of its retail deposit base.

Nobody had decided to run. Several hundred thousand pieces of software had independently concluded that a better rate was available, that the switching cost was nil, and that their instructions did not include a loyalty term. Each was correct.

"I keep having to explain to people that this was not a crisis of confidence," says Dana Whitfield, who was the bank's treasurer at the time and is now a consultant. "Confidence never entered into it. Our depositors mostly did not know it happened. Their agents did it, and their agents were right to do it, and the aggregate of ten thousand correct decisions was a bank with a liquidity problem it did not have at breakfast."

Two rules, one outcome

What happened to Buckeye Commerce was the convergence of two policy decisions that were made separately, for good reasons, by people who were not modelling each other.

The first was open banking. The data access rules that took effect from 2026 required institutions to give customers, and the third parties customers authorise, secure programmatic access to their own account information and the ability to initiate payments. The intent was to break the data lock-in that let banks hold customers through inertia rather than value. It worked exactly as designed.

The second was agentic commerce. Once software could hold a balance, evaluate a purchase, and settle it without a human in the loop, the wallet stopped being a place money rested between paydays and became a working position that an agent managed continuously. The agent buying your groceries has an opinion about which account those groceries clear from, and that opinion is refreshed every time anything in the market changes.

Neither rule anticipated the other. Open banking assumed a human at the end of the API, occasionally checking rates. Agentic commerce assumed the funding side was stable and the interesting problem was execution. Put together, they produced a retail deposit base with the behavioural characteristics of institutional hot money and none of the pricing that hot money used to carry.

"Confidence never entered into it. Their agents did it, and their agents were right to do it."

Deposit stickiness, the reliable tendency of ordinary people not to move money even when moving it would pay, was never a service. It was friction. It was the tedium of opening an account, moving direct debits, remembering the password, and getting around to it. Banks priced that friction into every funding assumption they had, and the regulatory framework priced it in too. Software does not experience friction. It experiences a switching cost, and open banking drove that cost to approximately zero.

The bidding war

The competitive response was, in hindsight, the only one available: banks began bidding.

Deposit beta, the share of a policy rate move that a bank passes through to its depositors, had for decades been the quiet engine of retail banking profitability. Through most of the 2010s it ran around 0.3 to 0.4 on the way up. Banks captured the majority of a rate rise and passed on a minority, and the gap was funded by the depositors who were not paying attention. By 2031 the blended figure across institutions with meaningful agent penetration is 0.94.

The lag figure is the one that treasurers find hardest to live with. A bank that repriced a month after its competitor used to lose a trickle of rate-sensitive money and keep everyone else. A bank that reprices four hours after its competitor now loses whatever its competitor's agents can reach in four hours, which on a well-connected retail book is a great deal.

So the banks automated their side too. Most large institutions and a growing number of regional ones now run counter-agents: systems that monitor competitor pricing, detect outflow in progress, and reprice automatically inside a governance envelope the treasury committee sets in advance. The deposit market has become, functionally, machines quoting at machines, with humans setting the boundaries once a quarter and watching.

"We are running a market-making desk against our own funding base," says the head of treasury at a $60bn regional, who asked not to be named because the bank has not disclosed the extent of the automation. "That is not what a deposit franchise was supposed to be. But if you quote by hand against somebody quoting by machine, you find out very quickly that you are the liquidity."

What the banks tried

The defensive playbook that has emerged is an attempt to manufacture, contractually, the stickiness that behaviour no longer supplies. Some of it works.

The insurance that does not bind

The deeper problem, and the one regulators have found hardest to address, is that deposit insurance was engineered against a failure mode that no longer dominates.

The guarantee exists to break a coordination problem. If your money is protected regardless of what anyone else does, you have no rational reason to withdraw ahead of them, and the run does not start. It is an elegant solution to a psychological pathology, and for ninety years it worked.

An agent does not run because it fears loss. It moves because a threshold in its instructions was crossed, and the thresholds people set are almost never about solvency. They are about yield, and about risk indicators that trip far above the level at which insurance becomes relevant. A ratings outlook change, two quarters of elevated charge-offs, an unusual movement in a funding metric: any of these can move money out of a fully solvent, fully insured institution, because the agent was told to optimise and nobody told it that a hundred percent insured deposit needs no defending.

The regulatory response has come in three parts, none of which touches the underlying dynamic. Agent platforms serving retail customers must now stagger and randomise large-scale movements, which buys hours rather than days. Institutions above a size threshold report agent-managed deposit share monthly, which gives supervisors visibility without giving them a lever. And the liquidity coverage framework has been recalibrated with new outflow assumptions for agent-managed retail balances, which is prudent and expensive and pushes the cost of the whole arrangement into lending spreads.

"You cannot regulate this away without telling consumers they may not use software to get a better rate on their savings," says a senior official involved in the recalibration, who spoke on condition of anonymity because the assumptions are still being finalised. "That is not a rule anybody is going to write. So we are left managing the consequences of a thing we are not willing to stop, which is most of what this job has ever been."

The dividend, and who pays for it

It should be said plainly that consumers have won here, and won substantially.

Households using agent-managed cash earn an average of 1.4 percentage points more on liquid savings than comparable households that do not. On a $40,000 balance that is $560 a year, transferred from bank margin to household income, and it is money that was previously being extracted through the mechanism of inconvenience. The competitive pressure has lifted rates even for the customers who never adopted an agent, because banks cannot easily run two prices.

The cost lands in two places. The first is lending spreads, where the funding cost increase is partly recovered, which means borrowers are paying for savers. The second is the smaller institutions that cannot afford counter-agent infrastructure and cannot match on price, for whom the choice is a shrinking deposit base or a funding cost that eliminates the margin. Community bank funding has shifted markedly toward brokered and wholesale sources, which are stable, contractual, and considerably more expensive than the inert retail money they replaced.

Buckeye Commerce survived. It drew on its facility, repriced within the day, and recovered most of the balances within a fortnight, which tells you something useful: the money was not fleeing, it was shopping. Whitfield notes that a majority of the deposits that left returned once the rate matched, and left again eleven weeks later when it did not.

"They come back," she says. "That is the part people miss. It is not a run in the old sense, because there is no fear in it and no finality. It is just that the money is awake now. It was asleep for a hundred years and we built the entire industry on the assumption that it would stay asleep."

The industry is now discovering what it costs to fund a bank with money that is paying attention.
