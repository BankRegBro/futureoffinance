The wallet woke up

At 3:14 in the morning, coins that had not moved since 2009 moved. More than a hundred billion dollars went to an exchange, and the transfer itself was the announcement: the mathematics protecting the financial system had stopped working, and somebody wanted that known.

The first transaction cleared at 3:14 in the morning, London time, and for about forty minutes it was a curiosity that a few hundred people on the internet were arguing about. An address funded in January 2009, holding a block reward that had sat untouched for twenty years, had signed a valid transaction. Then another did. Then eleven more, in a steady unhurried sequence, none of them in any hurry at all.

By 3:36 the outputs had been consolidated. At 3:41 the consolidated balance, a little over 1.1m bitcoin, was sent in a single transaction to a deposit address at a regulated American exchange. At the prior day's close it was worth a little over a hundred and ten billion dollars.

There is no version of this that is a theft in the ordinary sense, and everyone who understood what they were looking at knew it within the hour. The private keys to those addresses are not lost in the way a password is lost. They are gone. The people who held them are dead or vanished or were never identified, and the entire premise of the asset was that a key you do not have is a key nobody can produce.

Somebody produced them. That was the message, and it was delivered in the most legible venue on earth: a public ledger that anyone can read, using the one set of addresses whose movement no one could explain away.

"They did not need to publish a paper. They moved the coins. Every cryptographer on the planet understood the claim by breakfast."

Why the exchange

The choice of destination is the detail that specialists still argue about, because sending more than a hundred billion dollars of unexplained value to a regulated, surveilled, freezable American venue is not what somebody does when they want the money.

The exchange froze the deposit inside three hours, filed with FinCEN, and had Treasury on the phone before the sun was up in New York. None of the balance was liquidated. None of it has been. It sits, in litigation and in limbo, and the most widely held view among the people who have studied the transaction pattern is that this outcome was not merely foreseen but intended.

A capability of this kind is worth vastly more as a demonstrated threat than as a realised theft, and it can only be demonstrated once. Doing it through an exchange that would certainly freeze the funds converts an act that would otherwise look like criminal opportunism into something closer to a diplomatic communication. The sender established, publicly and unambiguously, that they could take anything secured this way, and simultaneously established that on this occasion they had chosen not to.

Attribution has never been formally established. Three governments have made statements that stop just short of naming a fourth. The interesting question was never who profited, because nobody did.

What the models actually did

The popular account, that artificial intelligence broke encryption, is wrong in a way that matters to anyone trying to plan.

The theoretical algorithms had existed since the 1990s. The obstacle was engineering: running them on real hardware demanded error correction so expensive that credible resource estimates ran to millions of physical qubits, and every one of those estimates rested on design choices that human researchers had assembled by hand across two decades.

Models trained on the physics literature and on simulator output proved extraordinarily good at exactly that search. Better error correcting codes. Shorter circuit decompositions. More efficient routing. Tighter distillation. No single result was spectacular. Compounded across the whole stack they cut the resource requirement by roughly three orders of magnitude, and a machine that had been forty years away became a machine that several national programmes already possessed and had not mentioned.

"We did not get a breakthrough," one of the researchers involved in the subsequent post-mortem put it. "We got two hundred small optimisations that all pointed the same direction, and the direction was toward us."

The planning lesson has been repeated since without much success. Timelines built on the assumption of human-paced engineering are not conservative estimates. They are estimates of one specific scenario, and that scenario quietly stopped being the relevant one around 2027 while the migration budgets stayed calibrated to the 2040s.

Bitcoin was the proof, not the problem

The asset was uniquely and irreparably exposed, for a reason peculiar to its design. In most systems a public key is a working credential that rotates on a schedule. On a public chain the key material is published permanently, by design, as the price of verifiability, and any output whose key has appeared is a standing target that cannot be recalled.

The remedy was obvious and impossible. Moving those balances to quantum-resistant addresses requires whoever holds them to move them, and the holders of the most exposed coins are dead, lost, or the founder. No authority could compel migration. No consensus process could resolve it in the days available. The market repriced on the arithmetic rather than waiting for a theft, which was correct and helped nobody, and what trades today trades as a collectible with an asterisk.

But the collapse of a single asset class, however large, is not why 14 November is taught in risk seminars. It is taught because of what the demonstration implied about everything else that was secured the same way, which was very nearly everything.

The trust layer

The banking system's first reaction was relief, and it was half earned. Its key material is not published. Its certificates rotate. It had a migration path, standardised years earlier, and the large institutions had begun walking it. Live customer traffic was re-secured with genuine competence inside eighteen months.

The relief did not survive contact with the second-order question, which is what happens to a system whose entire notion of authenticity rests on signatures that can now be forged.

Consider code signing. Every bank in the country runs software that its own systems accept because it carries a valid signature from a trusted publisher. The signature is the reason the update installs without a human deciding to trust it. An adversary able to produce a signature indistinguishable from the genuine article does not need to breach a network. It can present itself as the vendor.

The same logic runs through the certificate authorities that vouch for the identity of every institution online, through the attestation chains that let a hardware security module prove it is the module it claims to be, and through the authentication that lets one bank believe a message genuinely came from another. None of this is data confidentiality. It is identity, and identity was the thing that broke.

What followed was the largest coordinated revocation and reissuance in the history of the internet, conducted over a weekend by people who had rehearsed it only as a tabletop exercise. Two card networks paused a settlement window rather than process instructions they could not cryptographically vouch for. It held. It was close, and everyone involved says so.

The one piece of infrastructure that performed above its design was the supervisory plumbing. The continuous feeds phased in after 2028 gave the agencies sector liquidity and settlement posture in close to real time across the first seventy-two hours, and the response read as coordination rather than scramble in large part because nobody was waiting on a data call.

"Confidentiality was the problem we had prepared for. Authenticity was the problem we had."

The archive

The third exposure is the one that no migration can address, because it is a fact about the past.

Harvest now, decrypt later was never a secret. Intelligence services have understood since the mid 2010s that traffic which cannot be read today can be recorded today and read later, and that for information with a long shelf life this is entirely rational. The financial sector produces a great deal of information with a long shelf life. Network architecture does not change quickly. Credentials are reused for years. Internal system names, integration topologies, vendor relationships, payment routing conventions, and the specific structure of who authorises what: all of it ages slowly, and all of it was moving across links protected by exactly the key exchange that 14 November retired.

Recorded, and unreadable

Traffic captured under public-key exchange stays sealed while the exchange holds. Volume accumulates. Nothing is legible to whoever is holding it.

An archive of this kind does not open progressively. It opens at once, and the whole of it becomes legible on the same morning.

The consequence is not that adversaries read bank traffic today. Live links were migrated. The consequence is that whatever was recorded between roughly 2016 and 2030 and retained is readable by whoever retained it, and recording was cheap enough that the prudent assumption is that a great deal was retained.

"You have to think about it as reconnaissance, not theft," says a former Treasury official now advising banks on cryptographic inventory. "Nobody decrypts a 2019 session to find a wire they can replay. They decrypt ten thousand 2019 sessions and build an extremely accurate map of how a bank is assembled. That map does not expire. And every future operation against that institution is enormously cheaper because of it."

What migrated, and what did not

The migration has been the largest coordinated engineering effort the sector has attempted, and its record is uneven in an entirely predictable pattern.

Status reflects live systems only. No entry addresses the readability of previously recorded sessions, which migration cannot remedy.

The pattern is familiar to anyone who has watched a technology transition in this sector. What migrated fastest was what a large bank controls directly and replaces often. What has not migrated is embedded in hardware with a fifteen year service life, or supplied by a vendor with no commercial reason to hurry, or sitting in an institution that cannot fund the work.

Which is to say the residual exposure has concentrated, again, in community banks. A $400m institution running a core platform shared by six hundred similar banks migrates when its vendor migrates, and the vendor migrates when enough customers make it a contractual condition. The customers able to make it a condition are the ones the vendor least depends on.

The state actor question

The careful version is worth stating precisely, because the loose version circulating is both alarming and wrong.

No US bank has suffered a loss attributable to quantum cryptanalysis. On any credible assessment the capability is not being used to move money, and there are structural reasons to expect it will not be. It is worth more unrevealed than spent, and 14 November already spent the one demonstration available.

What the capability is worth now is position. An adversary holding a decrypted archive knows how an institution is assembled, which third parties it depends on, where its authorisation boundaries sit, and which of its systems were still running retired cryptography in 2028 and are therefore probably still running it. That is preparatory intelligence. It is durable, it is specific, and it converts directly into a shorter path for whatever comes next.

The supervisory response has been to require what almost nobody had: an inventory. Institutions must maintain and report a cryptographic bill of materials covering every system, dependency and vendor, with migration status and a dated remediation plan. It is unglamorous and it is the single most useful thing the agencies have done, because the recurring finding across two years of examinations is that institutions did not know what they were running.

"The first question we ask is what cryptography you use. Most of the time nobody in the building can answer it."

Nothing here suggests a defenceless sector. The migration was fast by the standards of anything this industry has ever coordinated, the replacement standards were ready before they were needed, and the institutions with resources have used them well. But the defender must migrate everything, including whatever came in with an acquisition eleven years ago that nobody has opened since. The adversary needs one path that was never migrated and one archive that was never deleted.

The coins are still sitting at the exchange, frozen, three years on. Nobody has claimed them. Nobody has explained them. They are the most expensive signature in history, and the message they carry has not been withdrawn.
