Nobody's on the file

Automated underwriting took the cost of originating a mortgage from $8,400 to under $200 and the time from six weeks to an afternoon. It also took roughly two hundred and fifty thousand jobs, and left a licensing regime with nobody in it to license.

A clean conforming purchase loan, the most standardised product in American finance. Faded figures are roles the file no longer passes through. Stylised, and drawn from the median rather than any one lender.

Renata Alvarez originated mortgages for nineteen years and can tell you the exact moment she understood the job was finished. It was a Tuesday in 2028, and a borrower she had been working with for three weeks called to say he had gone ahead and applied somewhere else over the weekend, on his phone, while watching television. He had a conditional approval before the programme ended. He was apologetic about it. He wanted to know whether she could match the rate, and she could not, because the lender that approved him had spent about ninety dollars getting to yes and her employer had spent nearly six thousand.

"He felt bad," she says. "That is the part I think about. He felt bad for me. He had done the entirely rational thing and he was embarrassed about it."

Alvarez was laid off fourteen months later, along with most of her branch. She now works in loss mitigation for a servicer, which she describes as the one part of this business that still needs somebody to pick up the telephone.

She is one of roughly two hundred and fifty thousand people who have left mortgage origination in the United States since 2027. The unique licensed originator count in the national registry has fallen from about 372,000 to under 120,000. It is the fastest contraction of a licensed profession in American financial history, and it happened not because of a crisis or a rate shock but because the work became cheap.

The arithmetic that ended it

The cost to originate a conforming mortgage had been climbing for two decades before it collapsed. Post-crisis documentation requirements, ability-to-repay diligence, appraisal independence rules and disclosure timing all added process, and process meant people. By the middle of the 2020s the fully loaded cost of getting one clean loan from application to closing sat somewhere between $8,000 and $9,000 depending on the lender and the quarter.

Almost all of that was labour. A single file passed through intake, processing, verification, appraisal coordination, underwriting, quality control, compliance review and closing, and each of those stages was a person with a queue.

What changed was not one technology but the assembly of several that had each been maturing separately. Direct account and payroll connections replaced document collection. Machine reading handled whatever documents remained, including the messy self-employed files that had always defeated the rules-based systems. Automated valuation, trained on far richer property data than the old models had, reached a confidence level the enterprises were willing to accept without an appraiser for a large share of purchases. And the underwriting decision itself, which had always been an expert system dressed up as a model, became an actual model.

The result is that a clean conforming file now costs a well-run lender somewhere under two hundred dollars and clears in an afternoon. Roughly seven in ten applications are never seen by a human being at any point before closing.

"He had a conditional approval before the programme ended. He felt bad about it. He had done the entirely rational thing and he was embarrassed."

The competitive dynamic was brutal and quick. The first lenders to reach the new cost structure did not bank the margin. They priced with it, because in a product as commoditised as a conforming mortgage the rate sheet is the entire marketing department. Everyone else had a choice between matching a price they could not fund and watching volume leave. Most tried to match, and the layoffs followed within two quarters.

The nonbanks moved first, as they had on every previous efficiency in this market, unencumbered by branch networks or by the internal politics of firing people whose managers sat on the credit committee. The depositories followed. By 2030 the distinction had stopped mattering, because everyone who was still originating at scale was originating the same way.

Who the licence was for

There is a question underneath all of this that the industry has been carefully not asking out loud, and it belongs to the state regulators.

The licensing regime for mortgage originators was built on a specific theory. An individual who takes a residential loan application, and who offers or negotiates terms, must hold a licence, pass a test, complete continuing education, submit to a background check and be findable in a national registry. The premise is that a consumer making the largest financial decision of their life is dealing with a named human being who can be held to a standard and, if necessary, barred from the industry.

That premise assumes the human is there.

When a borrower opens an application on their phone at eleven at night, connects their payroll and bank accounts, receives a conditional approval with terms and a rate lock, and closes eleven days later having spoken to nobody, it is genuinely unclear who took the application. Lenders sponsor a licensed individual who is nominally responsible for files originated through the platform, sometimes thousands of them a month. That person has never spoken to the borrower and in most cases could not identify the file.

Nobody thinks this is satisfactory. The industry position is that the licensed sponsor is accountable and the system is auditable, which is true and is not the same thing. The consumer advocate position is that a licence attached to nobody in particular protects nobody in particular. The state regulators, who run the registry and do the examinations, are somewhere in between and have been unusually candid that the framework is being asked to do something it was not designed for.

"We built a regime around individual accountability because individuals were what there was," a state mortgage supervisor told a conference audience last year. "We can keep issuing licences to people who supervise software. But we should be honest that we are licensing a job that mostly is not being done any more, and decide what we actually want to hold accountable."

The competing proposals are all imperfect. One would licence the system rather than the person, examining the model, its governance and its outputs the way an institution is examined, which is coherent and requires supervisory capability the states have been building unevenly. Another would preserve individual licensing but attach it to defined decision points, so that a human licensee signs specific determinations rather than a monthly volume. A third would leave the framework alone and let examination practice absorb the change, which is where things have drifted by default.

What stayed human

The work did not disappear evenly. What survives is what the model is worst at, which turns out to be a coherent category rather than a random remainder.

The pattern is that the machine took the file and left the person. Anything that is a determinable fact about a transaction has been automated. Anything that requires sitting with somebody who does not understand what is happening to them has not.

This has produced a curious inversion in what the remaining jobs pay. A loan officer whose value was volume is gone. A specialist who can underwrite a self-employed borrower with three entities and a bad year, or who can talk a first-time buyer through why the number changed, is worth considerably more than they were in 2024, because there are far fewer of them and the files that reach them are the ones the system could not price.

The access argument

It would be a mistake to file this purely as a story about disruption and loss, because the evidence on credit access runs the other way, and it runs further than most people expected.

The old underwriting framework was calibrated for a borrower who no longer describes a majority of the country: salaried, at one employer, paid on a regular cycle, with a credit file built through conventional borrowing. Applicants who did not fit were not necessarily worse risks. They were worse matches for the documentation the process demanded, and the process resolved ambiguity by declining.

Systems that read cash flow directly, that aggregate income across sources, and that assess the trajectory of earnings rather than their regularity are simply better at these borrowers. Approval rates for self-employed and multiple-income applicants at the largest automated lenders run substantially above what the conventional framework produced, and the early performance data does not show those loans defaulting at higher rates. They were being misclassified, and now they are not.

The fair lending question is live and unresolved and will stay that way. A model that never sees a protected characteristic can still produce disparate outcomes through variables that correlate with one, and the largest lenders now run standing testing programmes against synthetic applicant populations precisely because the regulators require it and because the exposure is enormous. The honest position is that automated underwriting has made disparate outcomes easier to measure and no easier to eliminate, and that measurability is worth something on its own.

The part nobody has tested

Everything described here has happened during a period of broadly benign credit conditions. That is the caveat that sits underneath the entire transformation, and the people who run these lenders say it more readily than you might expect.

The models were trained on outcomes from a particular set of years. They have priced risk accurately in an economy that has not seriously punished a mispriced mortgage since they were deployed. Nobody knows how a system that decides in ninety seconds behaves when the collateral assumptions move, because it has not had to.

There is also the servicing question, which the origination story tends to bury. A book originated at two hundred dollars a file is serviced by an operation that was sized for the same efficiency, and default servicing is the most human-intensive activity in this industry. Loss mitigation is a negotiation with a frightened person about the worst year of their life. It does not compress the way verification did.

Which is where Alvarez ended up, and why she thinks her current employer will be hiring in a downturn while everyone else is not.

"They automated the easy part," she says. "Getting the money out was always the easy part. Nobody has automated getting it back from somebody who does not have it."

"They automated the easy part. Getting the money out was always the easy part."

The mortgage market of 2031 is faster, cheaper and by most measures fairer at the point of decision than the one it replaced. The borrower gets a better rate and a shorter wait, and a meaningful number of borrowers who would have been declined are being approved and are performing. Those are real gains and they were paid for by a quarter of a million people who did a job that no longer exists.

The licensing framework is still there, still issuing credentials, still running examinations. It is a regime built to hold an individual accountable to a borrower, operating in a market where the two rarely meet. Whatever replaces it will be decided in the next few years, mostly by state regulators, mostly without much public attention, and it will determine what accountability means for the largest transaction most people ever enter.
